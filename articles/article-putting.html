AimPoint & The Putting Routine:
Reading Greens and Where to Focus

Putting accounts for roughly 40% of all strokes in a round of golf — yet most amateurs spend less than 10% of their practice time on it. The green is where scores are made and broken. Here is how to read it better, stand over it with confidence, and know exactly where your eyes should be.

What Is AimPoint and Why Does It Work?

AimPoint Express is a green-reading system developed by Mark Sweeney, used by dozens of Tour professionals including major champions. The core insight is elegant: your feet are more accurate sensors than your eyes. The human body is exquisitely sensitive to slope through its feet — far more than the visual system, which is easily fooled by surrounding terrain, shadows, and optical illusions on the green.

Instead of crouching behind the ball and trying to visually judge where the green slopes, AimPoint has you stand with your feet straddling the midpoint of the putt, feel the slope percentage through your feet (0%, 1%, 2%, 3% or more), and then use a simple finger system to find your aim point. The result is a consistent, repeatable read that removes guesswork and guessing from one of the most important skills in the game.

"Your feet don't lie. Learn to listen to them on the green and your reads will immediately become more consistent."

— Daren Lim, AimPoint Certified Instructor

How AimPoint Works: The 3-Step Process

AimPoint Express: Feel the slope percentage through your feet at the midpoint, then use your fingers to find the aim point above the hole.

01
Find the Midpoint
Walk to the midpoint between your ball and the hole. Stand with your feet straddling the line, perpendicular to the fall line of the putt. This is where you feel the slope — not from behind the ball where your eyes can deceive you.
02
Feel the Slope %
Close your eyes briefly and feel how much weight is on the low-side foot versus the high-side foot. A barely perceptible slope is 1%. A noticeable tilt is 2%. Significant — 3%. Steep — 4%+. This takes practice but becomes instinctive quickly.
03
Raise Your Fingers
Hold your dominant hand up at arm's length, facing the hole. For a 1% slope, use 1 finger. For 2%, use 2 fingers, and so on. Place your index finger on the centre of the cup — your outermost finger indicates your aim point above the hole.
04
Commit and Stroke
Lock in that aim point above the hole. Everything else in your routine — alignment, stance, grip — is built around starting the ball at that precise spot. AimPoint gives you the read; your routine and stroke do the rest.

Building a Bulletproof Pre-Putt Routine

The green is where routines matter most. Unlike a full swing — which happens too fast for conscious thought — a putt gives you time to overthink. A solid pre-putt routine fills that time with purposeful action, leaving no room for doubt to creep in.

The best routines are consistent in sequence and time. Tour studies consistently show that the most accurate putters take almost exactly the same amount of time from stepping up to the ball to striking it — round after round. That consistency is not a coincidence. It is a trained habit that prevents the hesitation loop: look at hole, look at ball, look at hole again, suddenly unsure, stroke falls apart.

The Complete Pre-Putt Routine

  • Green read (AimPoint): Walk to the midpoint, feel the slope, determine your aim point above the hole
  • Low-side read: Crouch low on the low side of the hole to confirm your read — this angle is the most revealing
  • Speed assessment: Look at the overall length, note the grain direction (shiny = with grain/fast; dull = against grain/slow), factor in uphill vs downhill
  • Practice strokes: Two practice strokes beside the ball, feeling the length of stroke needed for the distance — not rehearsing mechanics, feeling the pace
  • Set up to your aim point: Align your putter face to your aim point above the hole, then build your body alignment around the face
  • Look at the hole once, then settle on the ball: One final look to confirm your read, then eyes back to the ball
  • Trigger and stroke: Begin your stroke within 3–4 seconds of settling. Commit completely — no second-guessing mid-stroke

Where Should Your Eyes Focus When Putting?

This is one of the most debated questions in putting coaching — and the answer depends on what type of putter you are. There are three main schools of thought, each with Tour-level evidence behind it.

There is no universally "correct" eye focus point — but there is a best one for your putting style. Try all three and test which produces the most consistent starts on your intended line.

Option 1: Back of the Ball (Most Common)

This is the classical method and the most widely taught. You focus on the back of the ball — specifically the equator of the back — throughout the stroke. This promotes clean, square contact and is well suited to putters who are mechanics-oriented: they want to feel the stroke, feel the contact, and hear the sound of a well-struck putt. The risk is over-focusing on contact at the expense of pace and read.

Option 2: Looking at the Hole

Several Tour professionals, most famously Jordan Spieth, putt with their eyes fixed on the hole rather than the ball. The logic is compelling: basketball players don't look at their hands when shooting — they look at the ring. Shifting your gaze to the hole frees up your stroke to become more instinctive and tempo-driven, especially on medium-length putts where pace is the primary variable. Most golfers who try this report an immediate improvement in distance control.

Option 3: A Spot in Front of the Ball

Borrowed from bowling, this technique has you pick a spot on your intended start line — 15 to 30 centimetres in front of the ball — and focus on rolling the ball over that spot. It is highly effective for golfers who struggle with direction rather than pace, because it shortens the visual task: instead of starting the ball at a hole 5 metres away, you are rolling it to a spot half a metre away. The accuracy required is the same; the perceived difficulty is much lower.

Coach Daren's Recommendation

Finding Your Eye Focus

Spend 20 minutes on the practice green with each method. Use a putting mirror or alignment sticks to check whether you are actually starting the ball on your intended line with each approach. The one that produces the most consistent starts — not the one that feels most comfortable — is your method. Comfort comes with repetition. Consistency is the measure that matters.

Speed Before Line: The Most Important Putting Principle

Here is something most amateur golfers have backwards: speed is more important than line on almost every putt. This is counterintuitive. We agonise over the break, the aim point, the line — and then stroke the ball with wildly inconsistent pace that makes the read irrelevant anyway.

A putt hit with perfect pace but slightly off line will end up within a tap-in range the vast majority of the time. A putt on a perfect line but hit 2 metres too hard can leave a painful downhill comeback. Get your pace right first, then worry about fine-tuning your read.

The best distance control drill is simple: on the practice green, without looking at any target, stroke 10 putts in succession and try to make each one stop within 30 centimetres of the previous ball. This isolates pace from direction entirely and trains your feel fast.

Final Thought

Putting is a skill. It can be learned at any age, at any handicap, with deliberate practice. AimPoint will give you better reads. A consistent routine will give you a calmer, more repeatable stroke. And knowing where to focus your eyes will help you start the ball on line more often. Together, these three things will take strokes off your score faster than any equipment change or swing overhaul.
